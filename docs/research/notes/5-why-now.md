Fact file compiled 2026-10-28. Each entry: figure — who measured it — reading month/year — population — URL — caution. ## Key question 1: Hiring difficulty for small firms

### Takeaway
Small-firm hiring difficulty has eased from the 2021–2022 record (51% with unfilled openings) but remains structurally elevated: NFIB's September 2026 reading of 32% unfilled openings is 8 points above the 48-year average, 45% of owners (87% of those hiring) report few or no qualified applicants, and "labor quality or availability" is the #1 problem for the third straight month at 26% (vs a 12% historical average). The Fed's 6,525-firm survey has 46% of small employers naming hiring/retaining qualified staff as a challenge in the prior 12 months.

### Cited Findings

- 32% (s.a.) of owners reported job openings they could not fill in September 2026, down 3 pts from August and "8 points above the historical average" (i.e., ~24%). 27% have openings for skilled workers, 16% for unskilled. Net 17% plan to create jobs in next 3 months. — NFIB SBET September 2026 PDF, read directly — [NFIB SBET Sept 2026](https://www.nfib.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/10/NFIB-SBET-Report-September-2026.pdf). STRONG for "small-business owner sentiment," but it is a member sample, not a probability sample of all small firms. Caution: the series is sentiment about openings, not a count of vacancies.
- 45% of all owners (87% of those hiring or trying to hire) reported few or no qualified applicants in September 2026 (25% "few", 20% "none"); 51% of owners reported hiring or trying to hire. — same PDF. STRONG/sentiment. Caution: "qualified" is owner-defined.
- 26% named "labor quality or availability" as their single most important problem in September 2026, top problem for the third consecutive month, "14 points above the historical average of 12%" and "at a historically high level." Only 6% named labor costs (lowest since Dec 2020). — same PDF. Caution: NFIB revised the response label from "Quality of Labor" to "Quality or Availability of Labor" in 2026 (noted in the June 2026 Jobs Report footnote), so compare with pre-2026 readings carefully.

### Inferences
- The honest framing is "persistently elevated, not record": 32% unfilled openings is ~1.3x the 48-year average and labor quality/availability is the #1 problem at 2x its average, even while total openings have cooled from 2022.
- Applicant quality (45–51% few/no qualified) is a steadier and arguably more relevant pain point for admin roles than raw openings; it has stayed in the mid-40s to low-50s through 2026.
- Robert Half's admin-openings share (64% at firms <600 employees) plus NFIB's skilled-worker openings (27%) support the claim that small firms carry the admin hiring burden, but both are weak sources.

## Key question 2: Admin wage inflation

### Takeaway
Pay for office and administrative support workers has risen faster than the all-private average through most of the post-2021 period: the ECI wage index for the occupational group is up ~24.5% from Q1 2021 to Q2 2026 (147.2 → 183.2), with year-over-year growth peaking at 5.7% in Q4 2022 and still 3.2% in Q2 2026. OEWS median pay for general secretaries rose 26% (May 2019 → May 2025, $37,690 → $47,540) and for bookkeeping clerks 23% ($41,230 → $50,670). State minimum wage floors (Florida $15 on Sept 30, 2026; 17 states + DC at ≥$15 in 2026) push up the bottom of admin pay bands.

- The defensible "why now" wage claim: the nominal cost of a general admin seat is up roughly a quarter since 2019 while supply (employment in the occupation) has shrunk 9–16%, so the same back-office work costs more and is harder to staff.

## Key question 3: Demographics of the clerical workforce

### Takeaway
Clerical occupations are shrinking and old: BLS projects general secretaries −1.6% (−30,800) and bookkeeping clerks about −6% over the decade, explicitly citing software and AI, while every projected opening in both occupations comes from replacing leavers and retirees. CPS 2025 median ages are 47.9 for general secretaries and 49.8 for bookkeeping clerks versus 42.1 for all workers.

### Cited Findings
- BLS Employment Projections 2024–34 (TED, published July 16, 2026): Secretaries and administrative assistants, except legal/medical/executive −1.6% (−30,800); executive secretaries −1.6% (−7,900); legal secretaries −5.8% (−9,000); customer service representatives −5.5% (−153,700); claims adjusters/examiners −5.1% (−18,200); procurement clerks −8.7% (−5,400); credit authorizers/clerks −6.2% (−700); medical secretaries +4.2% (+35,300). BLS states "various types of office and administrative support workers are expected to see additional efficiency gains" as AI integrates into workflows, and attributes the declines for procurement clerks, credit clerks, legal secretaries and customer service reps to those gains. — [BLS TED, AI, IT and employment 2024–34](https://www.bls.gov/opub/ted/2026/artificial-intelligence-information-technology-and-employment-2024-34.htm). STRONG. Caution: BLS projections are deliberately conservative about technology adoption speed; they project employment, not task volume.
- BLS OOH (now on a 2025–35 base): Secretaries and administrative assistants overall 3,515,600 jobs (2025), −2% (−75,300) to 2035; ~314,400 openings/year, all from replacement (transfers and exits such as retirement); reason: "Technology, including artificial intelligence (AI) systems and digital tools," lets staff prepare their own documents; managers handle tasks secretaries once did. — [OOH Secretaries and Administrative Assistants](https://www.bls.gov/ooh/office-and-administrative-support/secretaries-and-administrative-assistants.htm). STRONG. Note: the OOH rolled to a 2025–35 base in 2026; the 2024–34 numbers above come from the TED article.
- BLS OOH: Bookkeeping, accounting, and auditing clerks 1,532,400 jobs (2025), −6% (−85,600) to 2035; ~144,100 openings/year, all replacement; reason: "Technological change reduces demand... Software has automated many of their tasks, so the same work can be done with fewer employees." — [OOH Bookkeeping clerks](https://www.bls.gov/ooh/office-and-administrative-support/bookkeeping-accounting-and-auditing-clerks.htm). STRONG. The 2024–34 figure was also −6% (via secondary; WageDex cites −5.8%).
- BLS CPS Table 11b, 2025 annual averages (11 months; October 2025 not collected): median age of all employed 42.1; office and administrative support occupations 42.4 (16,444,000 employed); secretaries and administrative assistants, except legal/medical/executive 47.9 (1,491,000); bookkeeping, accounting, and auditing clerks 49.8 (1,254,000); receptionists and information clerks 36.8 (1,247,000). — [BLS CPS cpsaat11b](https://www.bls.gov/cps/cpsaat11b.htm). STRONG. Caution: household-survey counts differ from OEWS establishment counts; median age ~48–50 implies a large cohort within 15 years of retirement but is not itself a retirement rate.
- Actual employment decline already observed (OEWS): 43-6014 from 2,038,340 (May 2019) to 1,706,790 (May 2025), −16%; 43-3031 from 1,512,660 to 1,373,680, −9%. — OEWS pages cited in Q2. STRONG.
- Older secondary evidence on age structure: ~31% of general secretaries and 51.6% of legal secretaries aged 55+ (JobsEQ/ARC regional table, undated) — [Atlanta Regional Commission](https://33n.atlantaregional.com/data-diversions/cracking-the-code-older-adults-in-the-workforce-part-ii-episode-5); Lightcast: 27.4% of ~223,000 legal secretaries aged 55+ — [Lightcast](https://lightcast.io/resources/blog/legal-secretary-a-dying-job-more-like-an-aging-job). WEAK (regional/undated/vendor).

### Inferences
- The combination of (a) all openings being replacement openings, (b) median ages near 48–50, and (c) a 9–16% fall in employment since 2019 means a small firm that loses its bookkeeper or office manager is drawing from a shrinking, ageing pool; the "replace the person" option degrades every year.
- BLS's own language now names AI as the reason for clerical decline, which is a citable, non-vendor validation that the work is automatable.

### Gaps
- No BLS series on retirement rates by occupation; the 55+ share for these SOCs from CPS Table 11b (the age-band columns) was not extracted in this pass and would strengthen the point.

## Key question 4: Falling cost of building custom software

### Takeaway
Evidence on AI coding productivity is mixed and setting-dependent (RCTs: +55.8% on a greenfield task, +21% at Google, −19% for expert OSS maintainers in early 2025, with METR's early-2026 follow-up still crossing zero), so the stronger "cost is falling" argument rests on input prices: the price of a fixed level of model capability has fallen 9x–900x per year (Epoch), e.g. GPT-4-class performance from $37.50 to $0.18 per million tokens (Mar 2023 → Feb 2025), and current frontier-model list prices are 3x below the 2024 Opus tier. Agentic tools are sold at $20/month entry tiers.

### Cited Findings

**Developer productivity studies**
- Peng, Kalliamvakou, Cihon, Demirer (GitHub/Microsoft), Feb 2023: controlled experiment, ~95 developers implementing an HTTP server in JavaScript; Copilot group finished 55.8% faster (95% CI 21–89%); less-experienced developers benefited most. — [arXiv 2302.06590](https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.06590v1). STRONG design, WEAK generalizability (one toy task, vendor-run). Caution: not a measure of shipping production software.
- Paradis et al. (Google), Oct 2024, ICSE 2025: RCT with 96 Google engineers; ~21% faster with AI features (96 vs 114 minutes), wide CI; authors warn against generalizing beyond internal tooling in summer 2024. — [arXiv 2410.12944](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.12944v3). STRONG design, single-firm.
- METR, July 10, 2025: RCT, 16 experienced open-source developers, 246 real issues; with AI (mainly Cursor Pro + Claude 3.5/3.7 Sonnet) they took 19% longer; they had forecast 24% faster and afterwards still believed 20% faster. Authors: "a snapshot of early-2025 AI capabilities in one relevant setting"; selection and learning effects acknowledged; page now marked out of date. — [METR early-2025 study](https://metr.org/blog/2025-07-10-early-2025-ai-experienced-os-dev-study/). STRONG (pre-registered RCT), narrow population.
- METR, February 24, 2026 update: continued experiment, 57 developers, 143 repos, 800+ tasks; point estimates −18% (CI −38% to +9%) for returning developers and −4% (CI −15% to +9%) for new recruits; METR says it is "likely that developers are more sped up... now" but its data are "only very weak evidence" because of selection effects (developers avoided submitting tasks they wanted AI for). — via [Birchtree summary](https://birchtree.me/blog/an-update-from-the-study-that-said-devs-were-actually-slower-with-coding-agents/) and [ValueAdd VC roundup](https://valueaddvc.com/blog/ai-coding-productivity-study-data-what-metr-mckinsey-and-github-actually-found-in-2026); primary post not fetched. Caution: secondary sources disagree on the sign; both CIs cross zero. Do not quote a speedup from this.
- Gartner (Magic Quadrant for AI Code Assistants, Aug 19, 2024): by 2028, 90% of enterprise software engineers will use AI code assistants, up from <14% in early 2024; by 2027, 25% of defects escaping to production will stem from lack of human oversight of AI-generated code. — via [IBM](https://www.ibm.com/think/insights/watsonx-code-assistant-software-development) and [GitHub blog](https://github.blog/ai-and-ml/github-copilot/gartner-positions-github-as-a-leader-in-the-2025-magic-quadrant-for-ai-code-assistants-for-the-second-year-in-a-row/). WEAK (analyst forecast seen through vendor reprints).
- Y Combinator: Garry Tan / Jared Friedman said ~25% of the W25 batch had ~95% of code written by AI; Tan also claimed the batch grew 10%/week in aggregate. — [CNBC via NBC](https://www.nbcnewyork.com/news/business/money-report/y-combinator-startups-are-fastest-growing-most-profitable-in-fund-history-because-of-ai/6188199/) (March 2025). WEAK (self-reported by an interested party; no methodology; Harness CTO publicly cautioned it ignores review/debug cost).

**LLM price declines**
- Epoch AI, "LLM inference prices have fallen rapidly but unequally across tasks," March 12, 2025: price to reach a fixed benchmark level fell 9x–900x per year depending on task; GPT-4-level GPQA Diamond performance fell ~40x/year. Examples (lowest price per 1M tokens at fixed performance): MMLU≈86: GPT-4-0314 $37.50 (Mar 2023) → Gemini 2.0 Flash $0.18 (Feb 2025); MMLU≈44 (GPT-3 level): $60 (Nov 2021) → $0.07 (Oct 2024); HumanEval≈67: $37.50 (Mar 2023) → Llama-3.1-8B $0.10 (Jul 2024). Authors: fastest declines occurred in the past year and it is "less clear that those will persist." — [Epoch AI data insight](https://epoch.ai/data-insights/llm-inference-price-trends). STRONG (independent research org, published method). Caution: measures price of a fixed capability, not spend; blended input/output pricing.
- a16z (Appenzeller), Nov 12, 2024, "LLMflation": ~10x/year price decline for constant performance; GPT-3-level from ~$60 to ~$0.06 per 1M tokens, 2021–2024. — [a16z LLMflation](https://www.a16z.com/llmflation-llm-inference-cost). WEAK-to-moderate (VC analysis, MMLU-based).
- Stanford AI Index 2025 (via secondary): cost of GPT-3.5-level (MMLU ≈64.8) inference fell from $20 to $0.07 per 1M tokens, Nov 2022 → Oct 2024, >280x. — [VentureBeat summary](https://venturebeat.com/ai/stanfords-ai-index-5-critical-insights-reshaping-enterprise-tech-strategy). STRONG source, but figure seen only via secondary; verify in the report.

## Key question 5: Step change in document capability

### Takeaway
Current vision-language models score at or above the human baseline on the standard document-QA benchmark (DocVQA human ≈ 96–98%; Qwen3-VL 96.5%; vendor pipelines 99%+), but handwriting is the honest caveat (≈90% on clean cursive, ≈64% on hard manuscripts in a Dec 2025 vendor benchmark). Government is deploying anyway: Pennsylvania's DHS has run ~400,000 client-uploaded documents through AI since Oct 2025 (30% flagged for quality issues), its Board of Pardons uses AI to transcribe handwritten clemency applications, and federal agencies reported 3,611 AI use cases in 2025 (up from 1,757).
