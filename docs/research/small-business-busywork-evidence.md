# Owners try AI; the paperwork stays manual

Every serious estimate since 2023 ranks office and administrative work as the most automatable work in the US economy. Goldman Sachs puts **46% of office-and-admin tasks** within reach of generative AI, and the Bureau of Labor Statistics now says outright that software and AI are why bookkeeping-clerk jobs are shrinking. Inside 5–50 person firms, though, almost none of that work has moved. In 2024 Census found only **3–4% of firms with 5–49 employees** using AI to produce their goods or services. On the Fed's generous 2025 definition, 46% of small employers "use AI", but only **7% of those users call it fully integrated**, which is about 3% of all employer firms. Keeping the work manual is expensive. A median admin assistant earns **$47,540** and a bookkeeping clerk **$50,670**. At firms under 50 people, benefits lift that to about **1.35× wages**, so a full-time admin seat costs **$64,000–$69,000 a year**. Pay for that seat is up about a quarter since 2019, while the clerical workforce shrank 9–16% and **32% of small-business owners** still have openings they can't fill. The measured results need the most care. Controlled trials show bounded drafting and support tasks getting **14–37% faster**. The best whole-office trial (66 firms, 7,137 staff), however, found a general AI assistant saved only **about 1.4 hours a week of email (2 hours for active users)** and changed nothing else measurable. The largest gains appear when a whole transaction moves off phone, fax and paper: CAQH measures a claim-status check falling **from 25 minutes to 7**. Owners who want to start mostly aren't stopped by cost. The top barrier they name is **"finding/adapting tools to meet business needs" (54% of small employers planning to adopt)**. That gap between dabbling and running the workflow is UnBusy's opening.

*How to read this report.* Each claim gives the figure, who measured it, the year, the population, a link, and a one-line caution on how far it stretches. **STRONG** means government statistics, peer-reviewed or academic work, or a large sample with a published method. **WEAK** means a vendor or sponsor survey, a small or self-selected sample, a secondary write-up, or marketing. Very little of this literature is cut at exactly 5–50 employees, so each row states the population it actually covers.

## 1. Admin work tops every automatability ranking, but small firms have barely started

The case that this work *can* be automated is strong and comes from many independent methods. Goldman's 2023 task model, the OpenAI/Penn exposure scores, Pew's occupation analysis and McKinsey's activity model all place clerical and administrative work in the most-exposed group. The O\*NET task lists explain why. The core duties of secretaries, bookkeeping clerks, data-entry keyers, receptionists and dispatchers are reading a source document, keying it into a field, checking it, filing it and scheduling off it ([O\*NET 43-9021](https://www.onetonline.org/link/summary/43-9021.00); [O\*NET 43-6014](https://www.onetonline.org/link/summary/43-6014.00)). The case that this work *is still manual* in small firms rests on adoption data rather than time studies. That data is unambiguous. Firms with 5–99 employees were the lowest and slowest-growing AI adopters in Census's 2024 series. Even after Census broadened its question in late 2025, firms under 20 employees "didn't change significantly" while larger firms pulled ahead. No study estimates automatability by firm size, and none should be quoted as "X% of a small firm's bookkeeper's week is wasted". These studies support only "X% of this occupation's tasks are the kind current software can do."

The trade-specific evidence is lopsided. Medicine, title and insurance have real measurements. Surveying, freight brokerage, CPA firms, property management and HVAC/plumbing have only anecdotes or vendor marketing, so for those trades lean on occupation-level evidence.

## 2. A busywork seat costs $64,000–$69,000 a year before the owner's own hours

The hard numbers on cost come from wages, not from time studies. BLS gives May 2025 medians of **$22.86/hr** for admin assistants, **$24.36/hr** for bookkeeping clerks and **$21.64/hr** for general office clerks; Florida runs 3–8% lower. The Employment Cost Index shows wages at **74.1% of total compensation at establishments of 1–49 workers**, so the honest small-firm loading is about **1.35×**, not the 1.4–1.5× often quoted. That gives about **$30.85/hr loaded** for an admin assistant and **$64,000–$69,000 a year** for a full-time seat. Hiring adds a median **$1,200** and **39 days** to fill ([SHRM 2025](https://www.shrm.org/topics-tools/research/2025-recruiting-benchmarking)). For pricing a desk, 30 hours a month of clerk-grade work costs about **$925/month loaded**. The same 30 hours of owner time, priced at the general-and-operations-manager median of **$50.85/hr**, costs about **$1,526/month** before counting any revenue the owner could have earned in those hours.

The *hours* evidence is weaker than it looks. No government survey measures admin hours by firm size: the American Time Use Survey doesn't code it, and no US survey of *staff* admin hours in 5–50 person firms exists. The two recent owner surveys are vendor-run and cover different populations. Taken together they suggest **13–16 owner-hours a week**, but those are derived figures and neither survey reports them directly. Use "roughly a fifth to a third of the owner's week, by owners' own estimate, in vendor surveys" or don't use it. The NFIB's own ranking is a useful check on overclaiming: federal paperwork ranks **#18 of 75** problems and cash flow **#37**, while locating qualified employees ranks **#5**. Owners feel the hiring problem more than they feel the paperwork. That points the pitch toward "the hire you can't find" rather than "you hate admin."

The other cost is money arriving late. Intuit's 2026 panel finds **59% of small businesses with invoices 30+ days overdue**, up from 47% a year earlier, with $17.7K outstanding on average. That figure is vendor data but from a large panel. The Fed's stronger survey finds **51%** struggling with uneven cash flow, a category that includes collecting receivables. Error rates have a peer-reviewed anchor, but it comes from clinical forms. On **handwritten** fields single-key entry produced **6.7 errors per 1,000 fields**, the closest analogue to retyping paper. For the "slow quotes lose jobs" story, the only rigorous study is a 2011 HBR audit, and its widely quoted figures were not verified against the full text.

## 3. Nearly half of small employers use AI; 7% of those have it built in

The headline adoption numbers range from 18% to 77%, and the spread is itself a finding. The broader the definition of AI and the more self-selected the sample, the higher the number. Census (nationally representative, "any business function") finds **19.8% of firms** using AI in May 2026. The Fed (employer firms, a definition that counts "features of software you already use") finds **46%**. Sponsor surveys from the Chamber, Goldman, Bank of America and Intuit report 66–77%. Every series that measures depth tells the same story: **7% of Fed users are fully integrated**, **14% of Goldman's program graduates are fully integrating**, Intuit's own payment data show **only about 1 in 10** businesses paying for a dedicated AI tool, and Census finds **57% of adopters in three or fewer functions**. The typical AI user writes marketing copy (83% of Fed users) and doesn't run back-office processes (21% use AI for process automation). Non-use also sticks. **71.5% of Census non-users** plan to stay that way, and **81% of NFIB non-users with 1–9 employees** have no plans for the next 12 months.

The "don't know where to start" gap shows up in two populations, and each needs its own story. The large group of non-planners says AI is **"not applicable to my business"** (65% in Census, 56% in the Fed survey). The Census authors call this a "perceptual lag": these owners don't see their paperwork as AI work. The smaller group that is interested but stuck (the Fed's **15% "planning to use"** firms) names **finding or adapting tools to fit the business (54%)** and **time to implement or train (37%)** ahead of **cost (32%)**. One precision point: among *current* users the top challenge is accuracy (46%), just ahead of finding/adapting tools (43%). "Top barrier" is exactly right for planners and nearly right for users. The literal phrase "don't know where to start" appears only in sponsor surveys: Goldman's 40% of non-users, undated and probably from 2024, and Bluehost's 12%. In government data the same idea appears as "lack of knowledge of AI capabilities" (22% in Census). Census also finds that needing a vendor or consultant is among the *least*-cited barriers. Owners aren't avoiding outside help; they don't know what to ask it for.

The document-heavy trades show the same shape: high "some use", almost no workflow integration. Clio finds 72% of solos and 67% of small law firms using AI "in some capacity", but only **8% and 4%** using it widely. Property managers report 58% adoption and **8% with any process fully automated**. Physicians are at 81%, and 57% name admin automation (documentation, prior authorization) as AI's biggest opportunity. For contractors the top AI use is **office and administrative functions (45%)**.

## 4. Bounded tasks get 14–37% faster; a general assistant saves about two hours of email

The controlled evidence follows a consistent pattern. When a person does one well-bounded task with a model alongside, the task gets meaningfully faster, and the gain is largest for novices: **14% more support issues resolved per hour (34% for novices)**, **37% less time on writing tasks**, **25% faster consulting tasks**, **12–32% faster legal drafting**. When a general assistant is handed to a whole office, the gain shrinks to almost nothing measurable. The 66-firm Microsoft 365 Copilot trial tracked 7,137 workers through telemetry for six months. It found **1.4 fewer hours a week in email (intent-to-treat) and about 2 hours for active users**, with no detectable change in meeting time or documents completed, and people used the tool in only about a third of weeks. Danish register data on 25,000 workers in exposed occupations, office clerks and accountants included, find **~3% of hours saved** and no effect on earnings or hours worked. Medicine's best-measured product, the ambient scribe, saves **16 minutes of documentation per day** in the largest controlled study, which the authors themselves call "modest."

Two further results matter for how UnBusy sells. First, perceived savings run ahead of measured ones. METR's experienced developers were **19% slower** with AI but believed they were 20% faster. A federal inspector general found GSA's claim of 240,000 bot-hours saved per year "inaccurate and unreliable." Self-reported "hours saved" should be discounted, including a client's. Second, the large measured gains come from moving the whole transaction, not from assisting a person inside it. The CAQH Index, with calculations by NORC across plans covering 63% of US enrollees, prices each manual medical-admin transaction against its fully electronic equivalent. A claim-status inquiry drops from **25 minutes and $13.80** to **7 minutes and $3.64**, prior authorization from **24 to 10 minutes**, eligibility checks from **16 to 4**. That 55–75% cut is the best public template for "before and after" on a document workflow, with the caveat that it was measured only in healthcare. No controlled study anywhere measures an end-to-end small-firm admin workflow (collect, read, key, prepare, decide). The evidence for the desk model itself is not yet published, and UnBusy will have to generate it.

## 5. Fewer, older, pricier clerks meet document reading at human level

The "why now" case is strongest where government data carry it. **Hiring:** NFIB's September 2026 survey has **32% of owners with openings they can't fill**, 8 points above the 48-year average. That's down from the record 51% of 2021–22 but structurally elevated. **87% of owners who are hiring** report few or no qualified applicants, and "labor quality or availability" is the #1 problem at **26%** against a historical 12%. The Fed finds **46%** of small employers struggling to hire or retain qualified staff. **Pay:** the ECI wage index for office and admin workers rose **24.5%** from Q1 2021 to Q2 2026 and still runs slightly above the private average (3.2% vs 3.1%). The median admin assistant went from **$37,690 (2019) to $47,540 (2025)**. One caution: CPI rose roughly as much over the same period (not verified here), so say "the dollar cost of an admin seat is up about a quarter", not "real wages surged." **Supply:** employment in the occupation fell **16%** for admin assistants and **9%** for bookkeeping clerks between 2019 and 2025. Their median ages are **47.9 and 49.8** against 42.1 for all workers. Every projected opening is a replacement. BLS now names "technology, including AI" and software as the reason.

**Cost of building:** the solid evidence is input prices, not developer-speed claims. Epoch AI measures the price of a fixed level of model capability falling **9×–900× a year**; GPT-4-level performance went from **$37.50 to $0.18 per million tokens** between March 2023 and February 2025. An academic counter-estimate puts frontier models at a slower **5–10× a year**. Current Anthropic list prices put the top model at **$4/$20** per million tokens against **$15/$75** for the 2024-era Opus tier. Developer-productivity trials are mixed: +55.8% on a greenfield task, +21% at Google, and −19% for expert maintainers of large codebases. The defensible line is that small new builds sit where the trials show gains, and that token cost is a rounding error next to a $48–51k salary. **Document capability:** vision-language models now match the human baseline on printed-document question-answering (Qwen3-VL **96.5% on DocVQA**, human ≈96–98%). Handwriting is the honest caveat: about **90%** on clean cursive and about **64%** on hard manuscripts in a vendor benchmark, and an academic study found traditional handwriting-recognition systems still ahead on historical documents. Government is deploying anyway. Federal agencies reported **3,611 AI use cases in 2025, up from 1,757**. Pennsylvania's human-services department screens client uploads and flags **~30%** as unreadable or irrelevant before a caseworker touches them.

## 6. What the evidence implies for positioning

The strongest controlled evidence doesn't support "give your office an AI tool." It supports the opposite. The best whole-office trial found a general assistant bought back **about 1.4–2 hours a week of email** and didn't change documents finished. Firm-level and register studies land at **~3% of hours**. Tools sat unused about two weeks in three. The big measured savings come from moving the whole transaction, as in CAQH's 25-minute claim-status check falling to 7 when nobody has to phone or fax. That is the case for a *desk* that owns one workflow end to end (collect the documents, read them, prepare the quote, filing or work order, stop at a person's decision) instead of a general tool the owner has to fit around the work.

The adoption data show where the opening is. Small employers have tried AI (Fed: **46%**), but almost none have built it in (**7% of users**, ≈3% of firms). The interested-but-stuck group says its problem is **finding or adapting a tool that fits the business (54%)** and finding **time to implement (37%)**, not price (32%). Census adds that hiring a vendor or consultant is among the *least*-cited barriers. That describes a buyer who will pay someone to make it fit. The larger non-planning group says AI is **"not applicable"** to them (56–65%). They won't be won by AI language, only by naming their own paperwork: the title file with 11+ documents, the 40 prior authorizations a week, the carrier portal they re-key into. That supports UnBusy's rule of using "AI" only when the owner raises it.

Three guardrails follow. First, **price against the hire, but frame it as the hire they can't find or the next one they don't have to make**, never as layoffs. Owner-reported labor effects of AI are close to zero (Fed 10% up / 11% down among the few who changed headcount; Census 2% of firms cutting AI-related jobs; NFIB 98% no workforce effect). Meanwhile NFIB's 32% unfilled openings and 87% few-or-no-qualified-applicants, plus BLS's all-replacement openings in a shrinking, ageing occupation, make "replacing the person" harder every year. Second, **keep the person's decision in the product**. Accuracy is the top concern of current users (46%; 75% of lawyers), handwriting is still well below printed-text accuracy, and the consulting trial showed quality *falling* on tasks just past the model's frontier. Stopping at a person's decision answers the objection buyers already raise. Third, **measure, don't estimate**. Every study that compared perceived and measured savings found perception ran ahead, and an inspector general threw out a federal agency's 240,000-hour claim. Before-and-after minutes per document on the client's own files, in CAQH's format, is the proof standard UnBusy can own, because nobody has yet published it for a small-firm workflow.

## Conclusion

The evidence changes the question UnBusy answers. Owners aren't short of AI. Nearly half have tried it, and the price of the underlying capability has fallen by orders of magnitude. What they lack is someone who takes one stream of paperwork and makes it run, and the data say that's where the measured savings are. General tools save an hour or two of email. Moving a whole transaction off phone, fax and retyping cuts its time by half to three-quarters. The labor market makes this urgent in a specific way. The admin seat is getting harder to fill and more expensive, so the pitch is to stop depending on a hire the owner can't make, not to cut staff.

The weak spot is UnBusy's own proof. No controlled study measures an end-to-end small-firm document workflow, owner-hour figures are all vendor estimates, and every study that checked found perceived savings running ahead of measured ones. The firms that publish honest before-and-after minutes per document on real client files will own the credibility in this category, and the field is currently empty.
